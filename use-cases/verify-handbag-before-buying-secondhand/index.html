How to Verify a Secondhand Luxury Handbag Before You Buy

A practical pre-bid checklist for buyers on Vestiaire Collective, The RealReal, Poshmark, Depop, and eBay. Five minutes of structured checking now beats a 14-day funds-frozen return claim later.

The scenario every secondhand buyer eventually hits

You found a Chanel Classic Flap Medium on Vestiaire Collective at $1,650. The price is good — Vestiaire Verified range for this model is roughly $1,650 to $1,950 — and the seller has six photos and decent reviews. You have 30 minutes before another buyer probably places a bid. What do you actually check before you commit $1,650?

The honest answer for most buyers is: not very much. You skim the listing photos, look for obvious red flags (uneven stitching, wrong color hardware), check the seller's rating, and bid. A trained authenticator looks at 22 specific details in a specific order — the same 22 details VeriBag runs through automatically in 30 seconds.

The 5-step pre-purchase workflow

Step 1 — Pin down the exact model

Louis Vuitton Neverfull MM is a different reference set than Neverfull GM. Chanel Classic Flap Medium has different documented checkpoints than Mini or Jumbo. Hermès Kelly 25 in togo leather is documented differently than Kelly 28 in epsom. Read the listing title carefully, then confirm the model variant from the seller's description. If the seller is vague about model size or leather type, message them before you bid — vagueness is a flag in itself.

Step 2 — Collect the seller's photos

Save the listing photos. The angles that matter for a 22-point check are:

  • Date code or interior tag — close-up, in focus, ideally with the entire stamp visible
  • Exterior stitching detail — a 6-inch section at a corner where stitch density and angle are visible
  • Hardware engraving — the engraved logo or model marker on a lock, clasp, or buckle
  • Glazing edge — the painted edge of leather trim, showing color and thickness
  • Monogram or canvas at a seam — for LV monogram and Gucci GG canvas, alignment at seams is documented
  • Back of the bag and base — overall condition plus base hardware feet if present
  • Heat stamp or brand mark — interior heat stamp typography and depth

If three or more of these angles are missing, message the seller with a polite request: "Hi, I'm interested but would love to see clearer photos of [date code / hardware engraving / interior tag] before I bid. Could you reshare those?" Sellers with authentic bags almost always respond. Sellers without authentic bags often go quiet — which is its own answer.

Step 3 — Run the 22-point reference check

Open VeriBag, select the brand and model variant (Chanel → Classic Flap Medium, in this case), and upload the seller's photos. The app does not require live camera capture for pre-bid checks — uploading existing listing photos is the supported pre-purchase workflow. The check completes in about 30 seconds.

Step 4 — Read the structured report

Each of the 22 checkpoints is marked one of three ways:

  • Consistent — the detail matches the documented public reference pattern for that brand-model-era
  • Flagged — the detail diverges from the documented pattern; the report shows you exactly what diverges
  • Unclear — the photo quality is insufficient to call it either way

Every checkpoint cites the public source it was compared against (Bagaholic, Purseblog, Lollipuff archives, Yoogi's Closet). A clean report typically shows 18–22 checkpoints consistent and 0–2 flagged. A suspicious report shows 4+ flagged checkpoints clustering around hardware, date code, or stitching — the three areas counterfeiters most often get wrong.

The report also includes a market price range from recent public listings on Vestiaire Verified, The RealReal, and eBay sold listings. If the asking price is wildly below the range, that is either an opportunity (motivated seller with an authentic bag) or a warning (the price reflects what the seller actually knows about the bag).

Step 5 — Bid, ask for more photos, or walk away

Decision rules that work for most pre-bid scenarios:

  • 18+ checkpoints consistent, 0–1 flagged, price in market range → bid
  • 14–17 consistent, 1–3 unclear due to photo quality → message seller for re-shoots of the unclear angles, re-run
  • 4+ flagged checkpoints → walk away, or escalate to Entrupy or Real Authentication if the bag is high-value enough to justify a paid scan
  • Price below the market range AND multiple flags → almost always walk away
  • Price above the market range AND clean report → fine if you want the specific bag, but you're paying retail-ish for secondhand

Red flags that show up most often

Across thousands of pre-purchase checks, the same four categories of red flag dominate:

Hardware finishing

Counterfeit hardware is the first checkpoint counterfeiters get wrong because high-quality hardware is expensive to source. Watch for engraving that looks shallow, lettering that's slightly off-font, or finishing color that does not match the documented era. On Chanel, the interlocking CC turnlock is the most common flag. On Louis Vuitton, the brass color and engraving depth on padlocks. On Hermès, the engraving on the lock plate (Made in France, year stamp font).

Date code or stamp typography

Louis Vuitton date code locations and formats have changed multiple times. Pre-2021 LV bags have a date code stamp inside; post-2021 production replaced visible date codes with embedded microchips. A "date code" on a bag claiming to be post-2021 production is itself a flag. Chanel date codes shifted format generations; the hologram sticker font has documented variations by era. Hermès uses a year stamp (blind stamp) with a documented font for each year.

Stitching density and tension

Authentic luxury production has consistent stitch density (stitches per inch) at corners and along seams. Counterfeit stitching often varies — denser in some sections, looser in others. The angle of stitch at corners is also documented per brand. Hermès saddle stitching is hand-finished and has a documented look that machine-stitch counterfeits cannot replicate.

Glazing color and thickness

The 2020-onward Louis Vuitton glazing (the painted edge along leather trim) shifted color slightly from the 2015 norm. A 2023-claimed bag with 2015-era glazing color is a flag. Chanel's lambskin glazing has a documented finishing pattern that counterfeit lambskin often misses.

Platform-specific notes

Vestiaire Collective

Vestiaire has its own Vestiaire Verified program (in-house authentication) for premium listings. For Vestiaire Verified listings, you're already paying a premium for their verification. For non-Verified listings — the majority of the platform — a pre-bid 22-point check is the difference between "I trust this seller" and "I know this bag matches documented reference patterns." Both can be true; the latter is the one that survives a dispute.

The RealReal

The RealReal authenticates items in-house and has been publicly criticized for inconsistent results across categories. For high-value items their authentication is the platform's product. A VeriBag pre-bid check adds a second opinion at minimal cost — if VeriBag flags an item The RealReal authenticated, that's a signal worth investigating before you commit.

Poshmark and Depop

Poshmark has Posh Authenticate for items over $500 (free third-party authentication after purchase, before they release funds to the seller). Depop has no built-in authentication. For both, a pre-bid VeriBag check on the listing photos is your only pre-purchase verification option. Buyers who skip it rely entirely on the post-purchase platform mechanism, which can drag for weeks.

eBay

eBay Authentication Guarantee covers certain luxury handbag listings (typically above $500 from approved sellers). For non-guaranteed listings, a pre-bid 22-point check is the buyer's primary defense. eBay also has the longest history of resale fraud across all platforms, so caution scales with deal value.

When a 22-point check is not enough

For bags worth over $5,000, for insurance claims, for legal disputes, and for estate documentation, a structured reference check is not enough on its own. Pair it with a paid physical authentication from Entrupy (~$99 per consumer scan, with certificate and financial guarantee) or Real Authentication (written authentication letter, $25–60 per item).

The compounding workflow that works for most active buyers: VeriBag for pre-bid filtering on dozens of candidates per month, Entrupy or Real Authentication for the one or two bids you actually move on at high value. See the VeriBag vs Entrupy breakdown for when each is right.
