How to Track Hair Loss Treatment Progress

A practical 12-month framework for finasteride, minoxidil, and microneedling — what to expect month by month based on the published clinical evidence, and why calibrated photos matter more than vague "hair scores"

You started finasteride three weeks ago. Or you added oral minoxidil. Or you began microneedling once a week. Now the question: "Is this working?" Most people answer that question wrong because they compare their hair in the bathroom mirror under three different lighting conditions and form a feeling, not a fact. This is the practical framework — calibrated baseline, repeatable photos, structured logs, month-by-month expectations from the published trials, and the dermatology check-in plan. Educational reference based on AAD guidelines, FDA prescribing information, and peer-reviewed PubMed studies. Not medical advice.

The Scenario

You are starting (or already on) one or more pattern hair loss treatments:

  • Topical minoxidil 5% twice daily (Rogaine® or generic)
  • Finasteride 1mg daily (Propecia® or generic)
  • Microneedling weekly (1.5mm dermaroller, evidence-supported per Dhurat et al. 2013)
  • Optional supplements (biotin, vitamin D, zinc)

Your prescriber said "see how it goes for 6 to 12 months and we'll re-evaluate." You want to know: is this working? Should I keep going? When should I worry? This guide walks through the 12-month framework.

Step 1 — Establish Your Calibrated Baseline (Day 0)

Before you take a single dose of anything, capture a baseline you can actually compare against. This is the single most underrated step in pattern hair loss management.

  • Five angles minimum: hairline (head tilted slightly back), crown (head bowed forward, phone above), front part (hair parted dry, photographed straight down), left temple (profile), right temple (profile).
  • Consistent lighting: same time of day, same room, same overhead light, dry hair (wet hair always looks thinner). Daylight near a window with no direct sun is the most reproducible.
  • Same camera distance and angle. This is where ghost overlay alignment in apps like Folik changes the game — your previous scan ghosts into the viewfinder, and you frame the new one identically.
  • Note your Norwood (men) or Ludwig (women) stage — your starting reference. Norwood I-VII for men; Ludwig I, II, III for women.
  • Save context labs if you have recent bloodwork. Total testosterone and SHBG matter for men on finasteride (DHT suppression has downstream metabolic effects). Iron / ferritin, vitamin D, thyroid (TSH, free T4) matter for women — deficiencies can drive shedding independent of pattern hair loss.

Without this baseline, you will spend month 6 trying to remember if your hairline looked like this in January. You will not remember. The baseline is for you, not for your dermatologist.

Step 2 — Months 1-3: Expect Shedding, Not Visible Growth

This is the phase where most people quit. The published evidence is clear: things typically look worse before they look better.

  • Minoxidil-induced shedding. Peaks weeks 2-8. The mechanism is documented: minoxidil pushes resting (telogen) hairs into the shedding phase to start a new growth (anagen) cycle. The shedding is a sign the medication is activating your follicles, not a sign of failure. The Blume-Peytavi et al. 2011 trial and FDA prescribing information for Rogaine® discuss this explicitly.
  • Finasteride at month 1. Finasteride works by inhibiting 5α-reductase, which lowers scalp DHT. DHT suppression takes a few weeks to register at the follicle level; visible density change lags by 3-6 months minimum. Kaufman et al. 1998 — the pivotal finasteride trial — showed first measurable density change around month 6, peak benefits around month 12-24.
  • Side effects watch (men on finasteride). If sexual side effects appear in this window, log them in the treatment tracker and contact your prescriber. The FDA-required label warnings cover libido changes, erectile function changes, and ejaculatory volume changes. Post-finasteride syndrome (PFS) reports describe persistence concerns that warrant attention rather than dismissal.
  • Side effects watch (everyone on minoxidil). Scalp irritation, dandruff, unwanted facial hair (rare). Oral minoxidil at higher doses: ankle swelling, palpitations, hypertrichosis. Severe cardiovascular symptoms — chest pain, severe edema, shortness of breath — are emergency: call 911.
  • Photo cadence: monthly. Same five angles, same lighting, ghost overlay alignment if your app supports it.

Do not stop because month 1 looks worse. Month 1 is supposed to look worse if minoxidil is doing its job.

Step 3 — Months 3-6: Stabilization Phase

The shedding should slow. Density change has not yet appeared but the trajectory has changed direction.

  • Shedding rate: most users report a noticeable decrease in shedding by month 4. The hairs you are losing in month 5 are different hairs than month 1 — they are catching up the cycle, and replacement hair is now growing.
  • Density: still mostly invisible to the naked eye. Pixel-density measurement on calibrated photos may show a 5-10% increase by month 6 (relative to baseline) for users on combined minoxidil + finasteride per published trials. This is below the threshold of visual perception in most cases.
  • Hairline movement: if you are working on the temples and frontal hairline, expect the slowest results. The crown responds first; the frontal hairline responds last and least to finasteride (this is well-documented and is a common source of disappointment).
  • Treatment adherence: the single biggest variable in 12-month outcomes is whether you actually used the medication consistently for those 6 months. Track adherence in the treatment log.
  • Microneedling cadence (if doing it): weekly at 1.5mm per Dhurat et al. 2013. More frequent is not better — skin needs recovery time. Pair with minoxidil 24 hours after microneedling, not the same day (minor evidence on synergy with timing).

Step 4 — Months 6-12: First Meaningful Comparison

This is the window where you can actually compare baseline against current and form an evidence-based opinion.

  • Visual density change. Pull up your day-zero baseline. Look at the same five calibrated angles at month 6 and month 12. If you have ghost overlay alignment, the comparison is honest. Most users on combined minoxidil + finasteride show measurable improvement by month 12 in the crown and mid-scalp. Frontal hairline lags or holds stable.
  • Norwood/Ludwig restaging. The Norwood stage is broad — Stage III in January and Stage III in December does not mean nothing happened. The within-stage progression (or regression) is where the action is. Pixel density measurement is more sensitive than discrete stage assignment.
  • What "working" looks like. Stabilization is a successful outcome — the AAD guidelines and most dermatologists frame holding the line as a primary treatment goal, not just regrowth. If your hair stopped getting worse, the treatment is working.
  • What "not working" looks like. Continued visible thinning on combined minoxidil + finasteride after 12 months of consistent use warrants dermatology re-evaluation. Possible considerations: dutasteride (off-label US, your dermatologist's call), oral minoxidil (off-label US), spironolactone for women, LLLT, or PRP. None of these are decisions to make from an app.

Step 5 — Month 12: Dermatology Check-In

This is the appointment that justifies the previous 12 months of careful tracking. Bring evidence, not a vague impression.

  • The photo journal. All five angles at day 0, month 3, month 6, month 9, month 12. Side-by-side slider comparing baseline vs current. Ghost-aligned photos that the dermatologist can trust.
  • Treatment adherence log. What you actually took, when, and any missed days. The honest answer matters here — dermatologists know that "I took finasteride daily for 12 months" is often "I took it 5 days a week for the first 4 months then twice a week for the rest."
  • Side-effect log. Any documented side effects with timeline. Especially important for men on finasteride documenting sexual function changes.
  • Specific questions to ask (3-5 max for a productive appointment):
    • "Looking at the photos and density measurements, is this trajectory in line with what you expect, or are we behind?"
    • "If we wanted to escalate, what would you consider — dose adjustment, adding a second-line treatment, or longer observation?"
    • "What does the AAD guideline recommend at my current Norwood/Ludwig stage?"
    • "Is there value in a trichoscopy assessment to see what density looks like at the follicular level?"
    • "What labs are worth running annually on this protocol?"

When to Contact Your Dermatologist Before Month 12

Most progress tracking can wait for the planned 12-month visit. These cannot:

  • Sexual side effects on finasteride or dutasteride. Libido changes, erectile function changes, ejaculatory volume changes. Call your prescriber.
  • Severe scalp irritation or signs of allergic reaction to topical minoxidil. Stop, contact prescriber. For severe allergic symptoms (facial swelling, breathing difficulty), call 911.
  • Severe cardiovascular symptoms on oral minoxidil (chest pain, severe edema, palpitations, shortness of breath). Emergency: call 911.
  • Signs of infection from microneedling (severe pain, pus, fever, red streaks). Urgent care or dermatology.
  • Sudden severe shedding not consistent with minoxidil initial-shedding pattern (e.g., handfuls coming out daily by month 4). Could indicate telogen effluvium from a non-pattern cause: medical issue, severe stress, illness, medication change, thyroid issue, iron deficiency. Dermatology evaluation.
  • New patchy bald spots with smooth skin — possible alopecia areata. Dermatology promptly.
  • Hair loss with skin changes — possible scarring alopecia, time-sensitive for intervention.
  • Mental health crisis from body image concerns or treatment-related mood changes. Call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline).

Important Boundaries

Everything above is educational reference based on publicly available AAD guidelines, FDA prescribing information, ISHRS resources, and peer-reviewed PubMed studies. It is not medical advice. It does not replace the judgment of your dermatologist or other licensed healthcare provider.

Pattern hair loss is not the only kind of hair loss. Alopecia areata, scarring alopecia, telogen effluvium triggered by medical issues, traction alopecia from styling, postpartum shedding, thyroid-related shedding, iron-deficiency shedding, and PCOS-related thinning all present differently and benefit from clinical evaluation. The framework above applies to androgenetic alopecia (pattern hair loss) management — the most common type — and does not generalize to other conditions.

Finasteride and dutasteride are FDA Pregnancy Category X — pregnant women must not handle crushed or broken tablets. These medications cause serious birth defects. Women of childbearing potential should not use these medications.
