How to Identify a Bug Bite on Your Skin

You wake up with three small welts on your arm. Or your kid has a row of red bumps on her ankle. Or you find a tick bite after a hike. Five questions narrow it down to the most likely culprit — and tell you whether to keep an eye on it or call a doctor.

Important: this is a reference guide for non-emergency identification. Any bite with severe swelling, fever, a bullseye rash, fluid-filled blister, rapid spreading, or any allergic-reaction symptoms (difficulty breathing, throat swelling, widespread hives) — call 911 or Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 immediately.

The scenario

It is 7 a.m. and Sarah is staring at three small red welts on the back of her wrist, arranged almost in a line. She has not slept well — she felt itchy around 4 a.m. but assumed it was anxiety. Her partner has no bites. Her dog slept in the other room. The bedroom is in a third-floor city apartment they moved into six months ago.

Is it a bed bug? Mosquito? Spider? Should she throw out the mattress, take her kid out of the bed, call an exterminator, or just take an antihistamine and forget about it?

This is the question PestSnap's Scan the Bite mode is built for. But the AI is not magic — it uses the same five pieces of information any decent dermatology resource would. Here is how to think through it yourself.

Five questions to narrow it down

1. How many bites, and what is the pattern?

Bite pattern is the strongest single clue, because different insects bite differently.

  • Bed bugs bite in a line or zigzag of three — sometimes called the "breakfast, lunch, dinner" pattern. They probe, do not like the spot, move a few millimeters, probe again. Two to three bites in a near-straight line is highly suggestive of bed bugs.
  • Mosquitoes bite in a scattered, random pattern. Single bites in unrelated locations, often on different sides of the body, with no spatial logic.
  • Fleas bite in clusters of 3-5 close together, almost always on the lower legs — ankles, calves, sometimes feet.
  • Ticks bite once, in one place. Usually you find the tick still attached or a single raised dot where it detached.
  • Spiders bite once, and most spider bites people think they have are actually something else. Genuine spider bites are rare in the US.

2. Where on your body?

Insects have preferences for skin location, and those preferences matter.

  • Bed bugs favor skin exposed during sleep — face, neck, arms, hands, shoulders. They hide under the mattress seams and emerge at night to feed; they do not penetrate clothing easily. If your bites are only on parts that were uncovered while you slept, that fits bed bug behavior.
  • Mosquitoes hit any exposed skin equally. Indoor vs outdoor, day vs night, head vs feet — they go for whatever skin is uncovered.
  • Fleas dominate ankles, calves, lower legs — they jump from carpet or pet bedding upward. Bites above the waist are unusual for fleas unless you have been sitting or lying on the floor.
  • Ticks attach in warm, hidden body folds — groin, armpits, behind ears, scalp, behind knees. They climb up from the ground until they find a covered spot.
  • Spiders bite wherever the spider was pressed against you — typically when you rolled onto one in bed or put on a shoe with one inside.

3. How big is the welt, and what does it look like?

The reaction size and shape often gives away the insect.

  • Mosquito welts are immediate, raised, itchy, and fade within 24 hours. Pink to red, soft, no central mark.
  • Bed bug welts often appear 1-2 days after the bite, are firmer than mosquito welts, with a darker center dot where the bug actually fed. The welt persists for several days.
  • Flea bites are small (2-5 mm), pink-red, often with a slightly darker halo. Intensely itchy but usually smaller than mosquito bites.
  • Tick bites are usually flat and unremarkable — a small reddish dot at the attachment site. The danger is not the bite itself but tick-borne disease, which can develop into a bullseye rash days to weeks later.
  • Spider bites from medically significant species (brown recluse, black widow) develop distinctive lesions over hours to days — necrotic centers for brown recluse, severe muscle cramping for black widow. Most "spider bites" people think they have are actually skin infections or bedbug bites.

4. When did the bites appear?

Time of appearance is your alibi.

  • Bed bug bites appear after a night in bed — and they appear after sleeping in the same room. If you sleep in a hotel and wake up bitten, it is the hotel. If you wake up bitten in your own bed and it happens every night you sleep there, your bedroom has the problem.
  • Mosquito bites peak at dusk outdoors or near standing water. Indoor mosquito problems point to an open window without a screen or standing water in a houseplant tray.
  • Flea bites accumulate after sitting on carpet or near a pet bed, especially if you have a dog or cat that goes outside. Bites often appear during or after the time spent in that spot.
  • Tick bites follow hikes, dog walks, gardening, or yard work in tall grass. You may find the tick still attached up to 48 hours after exposure.
  • Spider bites are random — usually no obvious time trigger.

5. Red flags that override everything else

Stop using identification apps and call a doctor or Poison Control if you see any of these signs:

  • Bullseye rash (concentric circles of red, fading toward the center) — possible Lyme disease.
  • Rapidly spreading redness (an expanding ring of warm, red, painful skin) — possible cellulitis or severe envenomation.
  • Fluid-filled or blackening blister at the bite site — possible brown recluse or severe reaction.
  • Fever, chills, headache, joint pain, muscle cramping within hours to days of a bite — possible tick-borne illness or severe envenomation.
  • Throat swelling, hives covering large areas of the body, or difficulty breathing — anaphylaxis. Call 911.
  • Bite on a child under two, on an elderly person, or on anyone with a compromised immune system and the bite looks unusual or worsens — see a doctor.

Back to Sarah's scenario

Sarah has three bites in a near-straight line on her exposed wrist. The bites appeared overnight; her partner has none; the bedroom is in a city apartment. The pattern (line of three), location (exposed skin during sleep), and timing (overnight in her own bed) all match bed bug behavior. The fact that her partner has none does not rule out bed bugs — about 30% of people have minimal visible reaction to bed bug bites.

The next step for Sarah is not to throw out the mattress. It is to look for evidence — Scan the Evidence in PestSnap, or just a flashlight inspection. Bed bugs leave rust-colored fecal smears on mattress seams, box spring corners, and headboards. If she finds those, she calls a licensed exterminator. If she does not, she keeps watching for new bites over the next week before drawing a conclusion.

One night of bites is not proof of an infestation. A pattern of bites with confirmed evidence is.
