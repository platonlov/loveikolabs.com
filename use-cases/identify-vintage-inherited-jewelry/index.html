💎 Use case · For inheritance recipients

How to identify and value inherited jewelry without a professional appraiser.

You opened the box for the first time after the funeral. Thirty-something pieces. Some look real, some look costume, most you can't tell. A jeweler quotes $75-150 per item for appraisal — that's $3,000-6,000 to know what you have. Here's how to do the first pass yourself in one evening, then send only the real items to a professional.

The math problem nobody warns you about

The average inherited jewelry collection from a grandmother or aunt contains 20-50 pieces. A certified appraisal typically runs $75-150 per piece. So a 30-piece collection appraised the conventional way costs $2,250-4,500 — before you even know whether anything in the box is worth the appraisal fee.

Most families respond to this in one of three ways, and all three are bad:

  • Sell everything to a pawnshop or "we buy gold" buyer. Result: you typically receive 20-40% of actual value, plus zero credit for collector or historical pieces.
  • Stash the box in a drawer for "someday." Result: pieces depreciate from neglect (silver tarnishes irreversibly, pearls dehydrate, opals craze), and you never deal with it.
  • Appraise everything and bill it to the estate. Result: thousands of dollars to discover that 70% of the box was costume jewelry worth $15 total.

There's a fourth path. Sort the box yourself first using AI scanning, then send only the high-value items to a gemologist. That can turn a $4,500 appraisal bill into $200 — and you keep dignity over your family's history in the process.

The one-evening sorting workflow

  1. Lay out the entire collection on a clean white cloth

    Good lighting, ideally near a window or under a bright LED. Take an overview photo first — useful for estate records and insurance documentation regardless of what you decide later.

  2. Pre-sort visually into three rough piles

    Pile A: probably valuable (gold or silver color with hallmarks visible, named maker stamps, gemstones with clarity). Pile B: probably costume (light weight, painted finish, plastic stones, no markings). Pile C: uncertain. Don't agonize — this is just a starting filter.

  3. Scan every piece in piles A and C with JewelSnap

    Three free scans before subscription, then unlimited at $4.99/week (trial available). Photograph front, back, and a close-up of any hallmark or maker's mark. You'll get material identification, gemstone analysis, era dating, authenticity verdict with confidence score, and market value estimate.

  4. Cross-check pile B with a few scans

    This catches the case where a piece looks costume but is actually a piece of high-value vintage costume jewelry (Bakelite, Trifari, Miriam Haskell, Eisenberg) — these can be worth hundreds to thousands of dollars at auction despite looking plastic.

  5. Make four final piles based on JewelSnap results

    Keep + appraise (anything estimated above $500 or with low confidence on something potentially valuable). Sell directly (genuine gold/silver under $500 estimated value). Donate or sell as costume (clearly costume with no collector marker). Sentimental keep (personal value regardless of market value).

  6. Take only the "appraise" pile to a gemologist

    Bring the JewelSnap PDF reports as preliminary documentation. The appraiser confirms or corrects the AI findings, which streamlines their process and may reduce their fee. You spend $200-600 on appraisal instead of $3,000-6,000.

What to look for in the box — beyond gold and diamonds

JewelSnap's era-dating feature matters most in inherited collections, because the highest-value pieces are often not the ones that look obviously expensive. Some examples that turn up surprisingly often:

Victorian mourning jewelry (1837-1901)

Hair jewelry, jet brooches, onyx and pearl combinations, miniature portraits. These were sentimental pieces made when a family member died. Many collectors actively pay $200-2,000 for good examples — but to a non-specialist they look creepy and worthless. A Victorian woven-hair brooch in a gold frame can easily clear $400 at auction.

Art Deco rings and bracelets (1920-1935)

Geometric patterns, calibrated baguette diamonds, contrasting metals (white gold with onyx, platinum with sapphire). The design pedigree alone can double or triple the raw-material value. A 1.5ct Art Deco diamond ring in a platinum filigree setting can be worth $8,000-15,000 — versus $3,000-5,000 for the same diamond in a modern setting.

Mid-Century costume by named makers

Trifari "Crown" pieces (1940s-1950s), Miriam Haskell pearl ensembles, Eisenberg sterling-mounted rhinestones, Hobé filigree, Schreiner asymmetric pieces. None of these are gold or precious gemstones — they're costume jewelry — and most are worth $50-500. Some Schiaparelli surrealist pieces from the 1930s have cleared five figures at auction.

Foreign hallmarks

If your grandmother emigrated from Europe, the Middle East, or Asia, the jewelry box almost certainly contains pieces with hallmarks you've never seen. A French Paris poinçon, an Italian Vicenza assay mark, a Russian kokoshnik — these instantly verify country of origin and metal purity. JewelSnap decodes over 50 countries' hallmarks. This is the single feature that most justifies the subscription for inheritance use cases.

Worth flagging: if anything in the box has an Interpol stolen-property database match, JewelSnap will alert you. This is rare but consequential — buying or selling unknowingly-stolen heirlooms can create legal complications. Better to know before you list it on eBay.

What to send to the gemologist (and how to brief them)

After the JewelSnap sort, send only items meeting any of these criteria to a certified appraiser:

  • Estimated value above $500
  • Confidence score under 60% on a piece that might be valuable
  • Anything with a maker's mark from a luxury house (Cartier, Tiffany, Van Cleef, Buccellati, Bulgari, Boucheron)
  • Diamond pieces above 1 carat
  • Anything you plan to insure or use for estate tax filings

When you bring pieces to the gemologist, hand them the JewelSnap PDF reports as preliminary documentation. Explicitly say: "I've done a first pass with an AI identification app. Could you confirm or correct these readings and provide a formal appraisal for insurance/estate purposes?" Most appraisers appreciate the prep work — it shortens their analysis time and often reduces the per-item fee.

What about pieces you want to keep but never wear?

This is the emotional half of the problem. Maybe the ring is real gold worth $300 but you'd never wear it. Maybe the brooch is costume worth $20 but it was your grandmother's favorite. Both decisions are valid.

Practical advice from people who have done this: photograph everything you plan to give away or sell, with the JewelSnap PDF as accompanying record. Years later, when nostalgia hits, you'll be glad you have the documentation. The portfolio function in JewelSnap is built specifically for this — every piece you scan stays in your catalog whether you keep the physical item or not.

One final note on emotional pacing

Don't do this in the first week after a loss. The sorting workflow above is mechanically two-three hours, but emotionally it can take a full day if the person who left you the jewelry was close. There's no urgency — pieces that have been in a box for forty years aren't going to depreciate in another two months. Wait until you're ready, then approach it as a focused project rather than a grief task.
