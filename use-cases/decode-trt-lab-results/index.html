How to Decode Your TRT Lab Results

A walkthrough of reading a testosterone replacement therapy lab panel — what each marker actually means in the context of clinical TRT, based on publicly available Endocrine Society and AUA guidance

You got back your LabCorp or Quest panel. Your prescriber's office sent the PDF with "all values reviewed, no concerns" in the portal. Your Total T is 743 ng/dL. Your SHBG is 18. Your E2 is 41 pg/mL. You feel bloated and your libido is down. Now what? This guide walks through every marker on a typical TRT panel, what each number means in context, and where the line is between "ask my prescriber at next appointment" and "call my prescriber today." Educational reference only — not medical advice.

The Scenario

Eight weeks ago you started TRT. Testosterone cypionate 100mg weekly, IM injection on Sundays. Your prescriber ordered follow-up bloodwork at week six (trough draw, Sunday morning before the next shot). You got the PDF back yesterday. You opened it on your phone, and the only context the lab provided was "WITHIN NORMAL LIMITS" next to most values. You feel different from baseline — better energy, mildly bloated, slight nipple sensitivity. You want to know what your numbers actually mean before your next quarterly call.

This is the most common scenario for new TRT patients and the moment most patients give up on understanding their own labs. Here is the framework.

Step 1 — Identify the Six Markers That Matter for TRT

Your LabCorp or Quest panel may have 30+ values. For TRT monitoring, six markers carry the most weight in clinical decision-making. Find these on your report:

  • Total Testosterone (ng/dL)
  • Free Testosterone (pg/mL or ng/dL, depending on the lab and method)
  • SHBG — Sex Hormone Binding Globulin (nmol/L)
  • Estradiol (E2) — ideally measured with a sensitive assay (pg/mL)
  • Hematocrit (% of red blood cells)
  • PSA — Prostate-Specific Antigen (ng/mL)

Some prescribers also order LH, FSH, DHEA-S, IGF-1, and a lipid panel. Those add color but the six above are the primary decision drivers per Endocrine Society and AUA TRT monitoring protocols.

Step 2 — Read Total Testosterone in Context

Total T is the headline number but it does not tell the full story alone. A few key points the Endocrine Society 2018 clinical practice guideline emphasizes:

  • Hypogonadism diagnosis threshold. Total T below 264 ng/dL on at least two morning measurements, with consistent symptoms, supports a diagnosis of hypogonadism per the Endocrine Society.
  • "Normal" reference ranges vary by lab. LabCorp's typical reference range for adult males is roughly 264–916 ng/dL. Quest uses a slightly different range. The reference range is a population statistic, not a personal target.
  • On TRT, the meaningful question is not "am I in range" but "am I in the upper end of physiological range without exceeding it." Most clinical TRT protocols aim for trough Total T in the 500–800 ng/dL band, peak below 1100 ng/dL.
  • Timing matters. Trough draw (just before next dose) shows your lowest level. Peak draw (24–48 hours after injection for cypionate/enanthate) shows the highest. Most prescribers order trough.

If your Total T is 743 ng/dL trough on week six of cypionate 100mg weekly — that is well within the typical clinical target range. The fact that your prescriber moved on without comment is reasonable.

Step 3 — Calculate Free Testosterone Using the Vermeulen Formula

Free T is the biologically active fraction — the portion of testosterone not bound to SHBG or albumin. It correlates more directly with symptom relief than Total T does.

  • The measurement problem. Direct Free T measurement by analog immunoassay (the cheap method many labs use) is notoriously unreliable. The Endocrine Society guideline recommends either equilibrium dialysis (gold standard but expensive and not always available) or calculation from Total T, SHBG, and albumin using the Vermeulen equation.
  • The Vermeulen calculation. The Vermeulen formula derives Free T from Total T, SHBG, and albumin using known binding affinities. It is well-validated and used in clinical research. Multiple free calculators implement it — including Calibrum's free-tier calculator, which uses Vermeulen by default.
  • Reference range. Typical adult male Free T by Vermeulen calculation falls roughly in 5–21 ng/dL or 50–210 pg/mL depending on units. On TRT, most clinical targets aim for the upper half of this range.

If your Total T is 743 and SHBG is 18 with normal albumin (4.3 g/dL), the Vermeulen calculation puts Free T around 17–19 ng/dL — comfortably in the upper-end clinical TRT target range.

Step 4 — Interpret SHBG in Context

SHBG determines how much testosterone is bound versus free. It is one of the most underappreciated markers in TRT.

  • Low SHBG (typically below 20 nmol/L) means more Free T relative to Total T. Often associated with insulin resistance, fatty liver, obesity, or exogenous androgen use. Low-SHBG patients on TRT often need lower Total T to feel optimal because their Free T fraction is proportionally higher.
  • High SHBG (typically above 60 nmol/L) means less Free T relative to Total T. Often associated with aging, hyperthyroidism, or genetic variation. High-SHBG patients on TRT may have "normal" Total T but symptomatic low Free T — and may benefit from a more frequent injection schedule to maintain stable Free T levels.
  • "Normal" SHBG range in adult males is roughly 10–57 nmol/L per LabCorp, but the meaningful interpretation depends on your symptom picture and Free T calculation.

SHBG of 18 in our scenario is on the lower end of normal, which is why the Vermeulen-calculated Free T comes out relatively high relative to the Total T number. This is typical for many TRT patients and not a concern in isolation.

Step 5 — Check Estradiol (E2) Using a Sensitive Assay

E2 is the most commonly misunderstood marker in TRT. Several points matter:

  • Use the right test. Standard "Estradiol" by immunoassay is designed for women's reproductive ranges and can give misleadingly high values for men. The right test is Estradiol "ultrasensitive" by LC-MS/MS or Estradiol by sensitive ECLIA. If your lab report says "Estradiol" without "sensitive" or "ultrasensitive," ask your prescriber if a sensitive assay can be reordered next time.
  • Reference range varies. LabCorp's adult male reference range for sensitive E2 is roughly under 39 pg/mL; Quest is similar. Clinical TRT protocols typically aim for E2 in the 20–50 pg/mL range, though optimal varies by individual.
  • "In range" can still be symptomatic. Some men feel best at E2 of 25 and develop high-E2 symptoms (bloating, nipple sensitivity, mood lability) at 35. Others feel fine at 50. The number is one input; symptoms are another.
  • E2 too low is also a problem. Aggressively suppressing E2 with anastrozole below 20 pg/mL often causes joint pain, lipid changes, mood issues, and sexual dysfunction. The Endocrine Society guideline does not endorse routine anastrozole use in standard TRT.

E2 of 41 pg/mL in our scenario is at the upper end of the typical sensitive-assay reference. Combined with the user's mild bloating and nipple sensitivity, this is a pattern that warrants a conversation with the prescriber — not necessarily a dose change today, but a "let's monitor this and see if it stabilizes" discussion at the next appointment.

Step 6 — Monitor Hematocrit (HCT)

This is the marker where ignoring the number can hurt you. Testosterone increases red blood cell production, and elevated hematocrit increases thrombotic risk.

  • The 54% threshold. The Endocrine Society 2018 clinical practice guideline flags hematocrit above 54% as a thrombotic-risk threshold requiring intervention. Above 54%, the standard options are: donate blood (most common), reduce testosterone dose (your prescriber's call), or temporarily pause TRT.
  • Trend matters as much as absolute value. If your baseline HCT was 42% and it climbs to 49% on TRT, that is a different conversation than if it climbed from 48% to 51%.
  • Hydration and timing affect the reading. Dehydration before the draw can artifactually elevate HCT. Drink water normally and avoid heavy exercise the day before.

For a new TRT patient at week six, HCT trend monitoring is the most important reason to do follow-up bloodwork on schedule. If your HCT is climbing rapidly, talk to your prescriber before it reaches the 54% threshold.

Step 7 — Watch PSA for Trend, Not Just Absolute Value

Prostate-Specific Antigen is the marker AUA prostate surveillance protocols watch on TRT.

  • The AUA guidance. The AUA testosterone management algorithm recommends baseline PSA before starting TRT and follow-up at 3–12 months. A PSA rise of more than 1.4 ng/mL within 12 months, or any PSA increase that prompts concern based on age and risk factors, warrants urology consultation.
  • Absolute value context. Adult male PSA reference ranges are age-stratified. Under 50: under 2.5 ng/mL is typical. 50–60: under 3.5 ng/mL. Above 60: under 4.5 ng/mL. These are rough thresholds, not diagnostic.
  • Velocity matters. A PSA going from 0.8 to 1.4 in six months is "still in range" but is a velocity that earns attention.

For most TRT patients under 50 with PSA under 2 ng/mL and a stable trend, this is a low-attention marker. For older patients or those with a family history of prostate cancer, the urology conversation is more active.

When to Call Your Prescriber Today (Not at Next Quarterly)

Most lab questions can wait for your next appointment. These cannot:

  • Hematocrit at or above 54%. Call your prescriber. Do not adjust your dose unilaterally.
  • PSA rise of more than 1.4 ng/mL within 12 months, or any flag from your lab. Urology consult.
  • Total T above 1100 ng/dL trough. Supraphysiological levels increase cardiovascular and prostate risk — your prescriber needs to know.
  • Significant unexplained liver enzyme elevation on metabolic panel. Could indicate oral testosterone hepatotoxicity or unrelated issue.
  • New chest pain, severe edema, or shortness of breath. Call 911 first, then your prescriber.
  • Severe mental health symptoms. Call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline). TRT changes can affect mood in both directions; this is documented and worth taking seriously.

Important Boundaries

Everything above is educational reference based on publicly available Endocrine Society and AUA clinical guidance. It is not medical advice. It does not replace the judgment of your prescribing physician, urologist, or endocrinologist.

Lab interpretation in isolation can mislead. The same E2 of 41 pg/mL means different things depending on your protocol, your baseline, your symptoms, your age, and your medical history. Your prescriber knows the full picture; the goal of decoding your own labs is to bring better questions to that conversation, not to substitute for it.

Testosterone is a Schedule III controlled substance in the United States. The use cases described above assume you are on legally prescribed TRT under physician supervision. If you are not, the right next step is a primary care visit and labs — not a tracker app.
